Power & Signal Board Design

A collection of production-intent PCBs designed for the ARUW competition robot — from high-current power distribution to precision sensor interfaces.

Altium Designer LTspice PLECS PCB Layout Signal Integrity

Overview

This page covers the PCB design work I've done outside of the ARUW robot overview — the individual boards, what made each one interesting, and what I learned from bringing them up. PCB design is one of those skills where you learn most from the mistakes, and I've made most of the classic ones at least once.

Power distribution board

The power distribution board is the most unforgiving board on the robot — it takes raw battery voltage and distributes it to motors, microcontrollers, and sensors. The motor loads are intermittent and can draw large peak currents during acceleration, which sends switching noise through the power rails and into anything sharing the ground plane.

The key design decisions were: separate analog and digital ground planes joined at a single star point near the power entry, wide copper pours for high-current paths (calculated using IPC-2221 trace width guidelines, then derated for thermal margin), and bulk capacitance on each motor rail to absorb current spikes before they propagate upstream. I simulated the worst-case transient in LTspice before layout to verify that the bulk cap sizing was sufficient and that the ESR of the chosen capacitors was low enough.

IMU sensor breakout

The IMU breakout is a smaller board but has stricter electrical requirements. The IMU communicates over SPI at several MHz, and at those speeds the traces start to act as transmission lines if they're too long or too close to switching signals. I used a controlled impedance stackup (targeting 50 Ω for the single-ended SPI traces) and kept the routing short and away from the power converter switching nodes.

The board also has a bypass capacitor footprint as close to the IMU's power pin as possible — ideally within a few millimeters. On an earlier iteration I placed the bypass cap on the far side of the board, and the IMU readings were noticeably noisier. Moving it adjacent to the power pin on the next revision fixed the issue cleanly.

Design process

My process for each board follows the same sequence: start with a block diagram, move to schematic with all component values chosen and simulated, do a design review before touching layout, route the board with the signal-integrity-critical traces first, then run DRC and visual inspection. The design review step — even just walking through the schematic myself with fresh eyes — has caught errors that would have caused a board respin.

Footprint verification is the step I'm most disciplined about now, after reworking a connector that had the pad spacing wrong on the first version. I check every critical footprint against the component datasheet before sending Gerbers. It adds an hour to the process and has saved me at least two respins.

What I've learned

The gap between simulation and physical reality is real, but it's smaller than you might expect if you simulate the right things. LTspice with accurate component models (using the manufacturer's SPICE model, not an ideal switch) gets you surprisingly close to what you measure on the bench. Where it falls short is in the parasitics from the PCB itself — trace inductance, via capacitance, ground plane resonance — which is why signal integrity tools and field solvers exist.

The other major lesson: thermal design is easy to ignore in simulation because simulators don't care about junction temperature. I now calculate worst-case power dissipation and check it against the component's thermal resistance to ambient before committing to a package size.
