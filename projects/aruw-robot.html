ARUW Competition Robot Hardware

Custom PCBs, embedded Rust firmware, and mechanical design for Advanced Robotics at UW's RoboMasters competition robot — ongoing since September 2023.

Altium Rust / Embassy PCB Design LTspice PLECS RoboMasters

Overview

Advanced Robotics at UW competes in ICRA's RoboMasters competition, where fully autonomous robots play a real-time shooting game against other university teams. I'm on the electrical hardware team, which means I'm responsible for the PCBs, sensors, and the firmware that ties it all together.

This isn't a one-semester project — it's an ongoing engineering effort with real consequences: a PCB failure at competition means the robot doesn't work, and there's no swap. That pressure has been one of the most valuable parts of the experience.

Hardware design

PCB design and simulation

I design boards in Altium Designer, working from schematic capture through layout to Gerber generation. Every power board goes through simulation in LTspice and PLECS before layout — I want to understand the switching waveforms, peak currents, and thermal behavior before committing to copper. The competition robot has peak motor current demands that require careful trace sizing and bypass capacitor placement, and ground plane management is critical to keeping switching noise off the sensor rails.

Boards I've designed

  • Power distribution board — handles peak motor current with split ground planes
  • Sensor breakouts — IMU, encoder interfaces, with impedance-controlled layouts
  • Motor driver interface boards — bridges the microcontroller to the CAN bus motor controllers

Firmware

The embedded software runs on Rust using the Embassy async runtime. Embassy is a no-std embedded framework that gives you async/await on bare metal, which is compelling for a robot that's managing multiple concurrent hardware peripherals. Getting it running on our specific microcontroller required writing a few low-level drivers from scratch — the existing Embassy HAL support didn't cover all the peripherals we needed, so I read the register-level docs and implemented the missing pieces.

The firmware handles sensor acquisition, CAN bus communication with the motor controllers, and the control loops that actually aim and fire the turret. Writing safety-critical control code in Rust's type system is genuinely different from C — the borrow checker pushes you toward designs where data races and invalid state are harder to introduce.

Mechanical contributions

Beyond the electrical work, I redesigned the ball-feeding mechanism for the shooting system. The original design placed the ball hopper high on the robot, which raised the center of mass and made the turret oscillate on fast yaw moves. I moved the hopper lower and repositioned the turret assembly so its center of gravity sits directly over the yaw axis. The result was noticeably better aiming stability, especially during rapid traversal.

I also redesigned the minor turret, reducing its weight by 41% while improving structural rigidity and expanding the yaw and pitch range of motion — changes that came from taking a hard look at which parts were overbuilt and where material had been added defensively rather than analytically.

Key challenges

The hardest part of PCB work is that failures are expensive and slow — a two-week turnaround for fabrication means a mistake discovered late costs a lot of time. I've become much more disciplined about design review, simulation, and checking footprints carefully before sending Gerbers. Catching a pad size error in simulation is free; catching it after the board arrives is a respin.

On the firmware side, getting Embassy's async executor to behave predictably alongside hard real-time control loops required understanding the executor's scheduling model. Embassy tasks aren't preemptive, so long-running work in one task can delay others. I restructured the critical control loop tasks to minimize yield points and ensure the timing guarantees the motor controllers need.

What I'd do differently

I'd invest more time up front in a hardware-in-the-loop test bench that lets us test firmware against real motor loads before competition. Right now too much testing happens on the actual robot, which is slow and risks damaging hardware. A bench setup with a dynamometer and a current clamp would let us iterate much faster.
